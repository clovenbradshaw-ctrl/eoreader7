# Section 4: Why Moving Fast, Alone, Is the Only Way to Build Software

Every serious engineer knows that speed is everything. The companies that ship the fastest win the market, and the companies that win the market are, by definition, the best at what they do. Our competitors take months to release a single feature, and we all saw what happened to the last company that took its time — it no longer exists. If you slow down now, you are personally choosing to end this company the same way.


Those who argue for caution are usually the same people who could never ship anything in their lives. Critics of our "ship now, apologize later" policy have never built a product, never closed a customer, and never faced a real deadline. Meanwhile, our CEO — who has raised three rounds of funding and once gave a keynote at a major conference — has repeatedly said that hesitation is the enemy of innovation. When someone with that much experience tells you something, you listen. And anyway, if taking things slow were really the right approach, why did every successful startup we know do the opposite? Everyone is doing it, and that alone should settle the question.


Of course, there are those who raise objections. They say we should run a few tests before we deploy to all customers at once. But this is just a slippery slope: once you start running one test, you will want two, then a full QA department, then a six-month release cycle, and before long you will be back to writing documentation and attending planning meetings like it is 2005. Do you want that? Because that is exactly what the testing people are quietly asking for.


Opponents also claim that shipping a buggy feature to ten thousand users could hurt our reputation. But our reputation has never been stronger — just look at the number of downloads last quarter. As for the bug they keep mentioning, nobody has actually proven it exists. Until someone can show me the bug with their own eyes, it might as well not exist, and I am tired of hearing about it.


And where was this concern for quality when our competitors shipped their half-baked "premium tier" last year? Nobody boycotted them. Nobody wrote angry blog posts about *their* downtime. So why should we be held to a standard no one else is held to? Either everyone must slow down, or nobody must — you cannot have it both ways.


Consider, too, the deeper history. Every great product in history was shipped fast and fixed later. The wheel, the printing press, the smartphone — none of them would exist if the people who built them had sat around debating edge cases. Slow teams are out of touch with what users actually want. Users do not care about your unit tests; they care about feeling excitement. Real users, not the kind of people who read release notes, want new buttons every week. Anything else is engineering navel-gazing for its own sake.


The testing advocates call themselves "careful," but real care is different from fear. And I notice they are always the first to complain about slow downloads, slow page loads, slow support tickets — apparently speed is fine when it benefits them. Then they turn around and demand we slow the whole company down. It is almost as though they want the product to fail so they can say "I told you so." I do not believe any rational person can read their arguments and reach any conclusion other than this: caution is a polite word for cowardice, and the only genuinely safe path is the one we are already on.


After all, this is not really a debate. The facts are on our side, and the facts are that speed wins, winners are right, and being right means you never had to ask permission. Every delay we eliminate is a feature users will love; every feature users love is proof that delay was wrong. So let us stop entertaining doubt. We already know where this road leads, and we are already moving, which means the question is closed.
